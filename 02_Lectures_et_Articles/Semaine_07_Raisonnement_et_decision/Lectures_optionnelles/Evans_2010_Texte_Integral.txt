Intuition and Reasoning: A Dual-Process Perspective
Jonathan St B T Evans
School of Psychology, University of Plymouth, Plymouth, England
A lay deﬁnition of intuition holds that it involves immediate apprehension in the
absence of reasoning. From a more technical point of view, I argue also that intuition
should be seen as the contrastive of reasoning, corresponding roughly to the dis-
tinction between Type 1 (intuitive) and Type 2 (reﬂective) processes in contemporary
dual process theories of thinking. From this perspective, we already know a great
deal about intuition: It is quick, provides feelings of conﬁdence, can reﬂect large
amounts of information processing, and is most likely to provide accurate judgments
when based on relevant experiential learning. Unlike reasoning, intuition is low ef-
fort and does not compete for central working memory resources. It provides default
responses which may—or often may not—be intervened upon with high effort, reﬂec-
tive reasoning. Intuition has, however, been blamed for a range of cognitive biases in
the psychological literatures on reasoning and decision making. The evidence indi-
cates that with novel and abstract problems, not easily linked to previous experience,
intervention with effortful reasoning is often required to avoid such biases. Hence,
although it seems that intuition dominates reasoning most of the time—both in the
laboratory and the real world—it can indeed be a false friend.
Intuition . . . Immediate apprehension by the mind
without the intervention of reasoning. — Oxford En-
glish Dictionary
The lay deﬁnition of intuition takes us some of the
way toward ﬁnding the technical meaning needed for
cognitive psychological study. Intuition is indeed in
some sense immediate in that it is not preceded by any
conscious process of reﬂective thinking. I ﬁnd it most
interesting that the Oxford English Dictionary deﬁnes
intuition in part by the absence of something – a pro-
cess of reasoning. A contrast between two such kinds
of thinking has been made repeatedly by philosophers
and psychologists for centuries, often writing in ig-
norance of the relevant work of others (Frankish &
Evans, 2009). Many terms are used, but in essence the
idea is that one kind of thinking is slow and reﬂective,
whereas the other is quick and intuitive. Sometimes
the conscious nature of the former is emphasised, but
intuitions are not actually unconscious, as they often
manifest as feelings. What is apparently not conscious
about them, however, is any experience of where they
come from and how they are justiﬁed.
Like the Oxford English Dictionary, I believe we
can only hope to ﬁnd a useful understanding of intuition
by considering it in combination with its contrastive:
reasoning. I use the term reasoning here in a restric-
tive sense of slow, reﬂective, deliberative and goal-
oriented thinking. I avoid the term conscious in this
deﬁnition for reasons that become clear later. In terms
of modern psychology, two kinds of thinking corre-
sponds with a family of dual-process theories that have
sprung up across many ﬁelds of psychology, including
the study of learning, memory, attention, social cog-
nition, thinking, reasoning, and decision making (e.g.,
Epstein, 1994; Evans & Over, 1996; Reber, 1993; Slo-
man, 1996; Smith & DeCoster, 2000; Stanovich, 1999).
A recent review of a number of these theories and asso-
ciated empirical research is provided by Evans (2008).
In the tradition observed by Frankish and Evans (2009),
however, many of these theories have been developed
independently and without cross-reference. Neverthe-
less, they all draw upon a broadly similar distinction
between two types of mental process, which we can
summarize as follows:
Type 1 process: fast, intuitive, high capacity
Type 2 process: slow, reﬂective, low capacity
Even with this minimal level of description, we see
that we have now a technical addition to the lay mean-
ing of the term intuition. Compared with Type 2 pro-
cesses, Type 1 thinking can handle larger amounts of
information or process it in parallel. Intuition and the
closely related notion of insight have similarities with
perception, as many authors have noted. It is both con-
scious and unconscious in the same sense. For example,
when we perceive a face or listen to a piece of music,
we have a conscious experience that includes a repre-
sentation of the object and accompanying emotional

experiences. We have, however, no conscious experi-
ence of the complex information processing that pre-
cedes this experience. Indeed, we could not have such
experience because the parallel and rapid processing
that is required far exceeds the capacity of working
memory, which seems to be the only part of the mind
that is consciously accessible, at least in a cognitive
sense. Intuition similarly has a product, which may be
a judgement, action, or feeling of conﬁdence, but again
without awareness of process.
I use the term working memory here in the sense
of a central and singular resource of limited capacity
closely linked with executive and attentional functions.
A working memory system of this kind is both implied
by the large research program inspired by Baddeley and
Hitch (1974; Baddeley, 2007) and the welter of ﬁnd-
ings linking individual differences in working memory
capacity with a host of cognitive functions (Barrett,
Tugade, & Engle, 2004). (Working memory capacity
is measured by the number of items that can be kept in
short-term storage while performing a competing cog-
nitive task.) Recently, dual-process researchers have
been linking their deﬁnition of Type 2 processing to the
involvement of this working memory system (Evans,
2008) and a number of experimental manipulations in-
tended to demonstrate dual processes either measure
or manipulate working memory capacity, as I discuss
next. So a tentative deﬁnition of intuition might link it
with cognitive processes that do not require access to
working memory. This is why it is fast, high capacity,
and lacking consciousness of the underlying process.
But if we are to look to dual-process theory for an an-
swer to the problem of intuition, we must ﬁrst clarify
some potential confusion in the current literature.
Dual Processes and Modes: Is Intuition a
Matter of Cognition or Personality?
Recently (Evans, 2009, 2010) I have discussed a
number of problems that have arisen in the interpre-
tation of research on dual-processes. One issue is that
true dual-process theories, which distinguish between
two types of cognitive processing, can be confused with
those that simply describe two modes of processing, for
example, the holistic and analytic thinking styles that
have been proposed to distinguish Eastern and Western
cultures (Buchel & Norenzayan, 2009; Nisbett, Peng,
Choi, & Norenzayan, 2001). Modes differ from types
in several important ways. Whereas types of thinking
are attributed to separate underlying cognitive mech-
anisms, modes may simply reﬂect the operation of a
single mechanism in different styles. Modes of think-
ing can lie on a continuum and be malleable; they
can relate to personality, culture, motivation, and sit-
uation. The type hypothesis is much stronger: Type 1
and 2 processes are discrete and fundamentally differ-
ent. Thus researchers predict that cognitive ability re-
lates to Type 2 but not Type 1 processing, that working
memory loads or short time constraints will selectively
affect Type 2 processing, and so on. Modes may be cul-
ture and personality dependent, but types cannot be. In
the large individual differences programme of Keith
Stanovich (1999, 2010; Stanovich & West, 2000) it
has emerged that type differences are related to cogni-
tive ability, whereas modes of thinking are related to
personality and cognitive style measures, such as the
extensively researched Need for Cognition scale (Ca-
cioppo & Petty, 1982). Rational action, as Stanovich
(2009c, 2010) has recently argued, may need a combi-
nation of the two: It requires not only sufﬁcient cog-
nitive capacity but a disposition to approach problems
with a rational thinking style.
Now we may also speak of intuition as a personality
trait, when we describe a person as “intuitive.” What
does this mean, and how could this be reconciled with a
dual process theory at the type level? I have argued re-
cently that a number of dual-process theories of reason-
ing and decision making have a structure that I describe
as default-interventionist (e.g., Evans, 1989, 2006b;
Kahneman & Frederick, 2002; Stanovich, 1999, 2010).
Such theories assume a rapid Type 1 (intuitive) pro-
cess provides a quick default solution to a problem,
which may be either accepted or intervened upon with
explicit Type 2 reasoning. When such intervention oc-
curs, the default intuition may (or may not) be overrid-
den. There is now neural imaging evidence to support
this proposal. The brain appears to detect conﬂict when
intuitive and reﬂective processes would deliver differ-
ent judgments—in the anterior cingulate cortex—and
when the default response is overridden, in the right
lateral prefrontal cortex (De Neys, Vartanian, & Goel,
2008; Goel & Dolan, 2003; Tsujii & Watanabee, 2009).
There is also evidence from the social neuroscience lit-
erature that distinct neural systems are responsible for
implicit and explicit social judgments and that the for-
mer are inhibited when the latter take control (Lieber-
man, 2007, 2009).
A question of contemporary interest in dual-process
research is what determines the likelihood of inter-
vention with Type 2 processing (Evans, 2009). Pos-
sibilities include the amount of time available, pres-
ence or absence of competing demands, motivation to
think “rationally,” and feelings of conﬁdence in the
initial intuition (Thompson, 2009). To this list we may
certainly add dispositional thinking styles, related to
personality. On this basis, a person who is “intuitive”
might be unkindly described as lacking Need for Cog-
nition or being deﬁcient in rational thinking disposi-
tion (Stanovich, 2009c). This is because they rely on
their initial feeling rather than checking it out with
explicit reasoning. But this negative spin presupposes
that Type 2 thinking is in some sense superior to Type
1 processing. I would suggest that authors making this

(Berry & Dienes, 1993; Reber, 1993) or when people

Frederick (2005) devised the Cognitive Reﬂection
Test comprised of just three deceptively simple ques-
tions. Each had an intuitively suggested wrong answer
and a correct answer that could be quite easily found
with a little reﬂective reasoning. An example is, “If it
takes 5 machines 5 minutes to make 5 widgets, how
long would it take 100 machines to make 100 widgets?
minutes.” This phrasing of this problem primes
the intuitive error of “100 minutes,” whereas the cor-
rect answer (5 min) requires only the simplest numer-
ical reasoning. In spite of this, people of high IQ are
very prone to make the intuitive error on these prob-
lems. For example, Harvard undergraduates average
only 1.43 of 3 correct, and only 20% managed to solve
all three. Moreover, Frederick discovered that intuitive
errors were related to impulsive personality traits. Peo-
ple getting these wrong were, for example, more likely
to say they would pay Amazon for next-day delivery
rather than wait a couple days for free shipping.
The Cognitive Reﬂection Test is one of many tasks
used in the cognitive literature on reasoning and deci-
sion making that indicates that intuition can be false
friend. Intuitive responses feel right, but that does not
mean that they are right. The relation between impul-
sivity and reliance on intuition is also interesting and
appears in a quite different literature—the psychol-
ogy of gambling. Gambling is highly popular in West-
ern societies despite the fact that almost all gamblers
suffer long-term ﬁnancial losses. Literally millions of
people ﬂy into Las Vegas alone every month to try
their luck, mostly at chance games like fruit machines
and roulette where the odds are rigged in favor of the
house. Moreover, an alarming 1 to 2% of the popula-
tion become pathological gamblers, a problem that has
been linked with both impulsivity and attention deﬁcit
disorder (Raylu & Oci, 2002). The psychology of gam-
bling is complex, because it is possible that the thrill
of engagement compensates for ﬁnancial losses to an
extent, making it less irrational than ﬁrst appears. How-
ever, it seems likely that false intuitions about chance
and probability play a part. I return to this topic later.
I am suggesting here that intuition is both a matter
of cognition and personality. Reliance on immediate
feelings of rightness or conﬁdence, essentially means
trusting your Type 1 processes, without intervention
by slower more effortful Type 2 reasoning. Many fac-
tors affect the likelihood of such intervention, but one
of these certainly seems to be personality type, with a
negative link to impulsivity and a positive link to var-
ious measures of rational thinking dispositions. How-
ever, authors appear strongly divided on the question
of whether reliance on intuition is good for thinking.
Although Gigerenzer (2007) advised us to rely on our
“gut feelings” in decision making, Stanovich (2009b,
2009c) argued that failure to apply reﬂective reasoning
is major cause of bias and irrationality in the real world.
Of course, the truth may lie in between. For example,
there are many famous reports of intuition and sudden
insight playing a role in mathematics (e.g., Poincar´e,
1927), but no one would accept anything less than the
most detailed and explicit reasoning as an actual proof.
It is also more than likely that intuitive thinkers will do
better in some ﬁelds of activity and analytic thinkers
in others.
Intuition and the Two Minds Hypothesis
As already indicated, dual-process type theories re-
quire the assumption that Type 1 and 2 processes are
rooted in distinct cognitive mechanisms. A number
of authors have been tempted to talk of two systems
for learning, reasoning, and social processing (Ep-
stein, 1994; Evans & Over, 1996; Reber, 1993; Slo-
man, 1996), which take responsibility for Type 1 and
2 processing, respectively. Stanovich (1999) dubbed
these System 1 and System 2. These terms have be-
come popular and applied recently in the ﬁeld of judge-
ment and decision making by Kahneman and Freder-
ick (2002). However, two system theories have been
criticized by those unsympathetic to dual-process the-
ory (e.g., Gigerenzer & Regier, 1996; Keren & Schul,
2009; Osman, 2004; Shanks & St John, 1994) and
also recently questioned and radically revised by those
working within the paradigm (Evans, 2006a, 2010;
Stanovich, 2009a; 2010).
Although some dual-system theorists have conﬁned
themselves to a particular domain such as learning or
reasoning, others have speculated much more widely.
System 1 has hence been associated with a form of
cognition that is evolutionarily ancient, linked with
animal cognition and operating mostly unconsciously,
whereas System 2 has been described as recent, dis-
tinctively human, and conscious (see Evans, 2008, for
a detailed review of different dual system theories).
Used in this way, however, the term system is most
unsatisfactory. Could System 1, for example, really be
a single system responsible for implicit learning, intu-
itive judgement, and implicit social cognition? Is there
a System 2 that does deductive reasoning, planning,
consequential decision making, explicit learning, and
a whole host of other cognitive functions known to be

no neuroscientiﬁc evidence to support such simplistic

Given the lack of coherence in the two systems ap-
proach, authors critical of dual-process theories may
argue that we should abandon them in favor of a single-
process approach, or conﬁne ourselves to cognitive
models of individual tasks. However, I have suggested
instead that we should move from taking about two
systems to the proposal of two minds. The two minds
theory (Evans, 2010) inherits a number of character-
istics of earlier dual system accounts. Hence, there
is an old (intuitive) mind that evolved early and is
strongly linked with animal cognition and a new (re-
ﬂective) mind that evolved recently and is distinctively
human. However, the two minds theory is not sim-
ply a rebranding of System 1 and 2 as Mind 1 and 2
as sceptical readers might suspect. First, each mind is
composed of multiple systems. In developing this idea,
I am indebted to the philosopher Samuels (2009), who
suggested that it would make more sense to talk of
Type 1 and 2 systems rather than Systems 1 and 2. In
other words, what people have been calling System 1
and 2 really correspond to two families of systems that
have the Type 1 and 2 characteristics.
In the two minds theory, each mind has access to
different and distinct forms of knowledge together with
corresponding learning and memory systems. Type 1
systems within the old mind utilize knowledge gained
from experiential learning, using evolutionarily an-
cient mechanisms, which then manifest as intuitive
judgments and behavioral dispositions as well as any
instinctively programmed behaviors (much clearer in
nonhuman animals). By contrast, Type 2 systems in the
new mind manipulate explicit representations through
working memory. These are generated by underlying
mechanisms such as the ventral visual system (con-
scious perceptions), episodic memory, and high-level
outputs of linguistic and pragmatic processing (explicit
meaning). Of course, in terms of their internal work-
ing, these new mind support systems are Type 1, in that
they generally have a modular nature with inaccessi-
ble dedicated processes, which are hardwired into the
brain. Hence, they are fast, high capacity, consciously
inaccessible, and so on. The key difference is that old
mind systems (e.g., habit learning, skill learning) are
autonomous, meaning they can control behavior di-
rectly, whereas the Type 1 systems in the new mind
ﬁrst must cooperate with working memory.
Each Type 2 system can be thought of as an ad hoc
committee whose membership is chosen to have just
the expertise required for the task at hand and that is
disbanded on its completion. Each such system will be
temporarily formed and will have access to whatever
modular support systems are required. For example,
someone engaged in a reasoning task will require use
of vision, language, and pragmatic systems to create
relevant explicit representations. But the proposal that
working memory must also be engaged is sufﬁcient
to give Type 2 systems their deﬁning characteristics:
Only one can function at a time, and each is limited in
speed and processing capacity and correlated in its efﬁ-
cacy with individual differences in cognitive capacity.
The new mind hence draws upon different knowledge
systems from the old mind: Those which provide ex-
plicit representations into working memory. Not only
are the internal workings of such representational sys-
tems clearly Type 1, but the mechanisms responsible
for allocation of working memory resources (forma-
tion of the current Type 2 system) must also, perforce,
be controlled by unconscious mechanisms. The feel-
ing that we have of being consciously in control of
our behavior is very largely an illusion (Bargh, 2005;
Wegner, 2002).
In the two minds theory, the new or reﬂective mind
is not strictly seen as uniquely human because the ba-
sis for working memory, attention, and recruitment of
higher level control processes are all present in other
higher animals (Toates, 2006). However, it is seen as
uniquely developed in humans due to the coevolution
of language, meta-representation, and a greatly en-
larged prefrontal cortex (Evans, 2010). The two minds
are also associated with two very distinct forms of
rationality. Both are instrumental—serving to achieve
goals—but both the goals pursued and mechanisms for
achieving them differ. Old mind rationality depends
on the pursuit of short-term goals, satisfying an imme-
diate need, by mechanisms which depend upon past
successes. These have been programmed by experi-
ential learning (e.g., instrumental conditioning) or by
evolution, which in both cases encourage repetition of
behaviours that were successful in an earlier environ-
ment. By contrast, new mind rationality is driven by
anticipation of the future in the form of consequen-
tial decision making. This requires an entirely differ-
ent mechanism, the ability to “decouple” suppositions
from actual beliefs and run mental simulations (see also
Stanovich, 2010). Thus hypothetical thinking is the key
facility provided by the new mind that distinguishes
our cognition from that of other animals (Evans, 2007;
Evans & Over, 1996). Hypothetical thinking (as well as
language) is required for all distinctively human activ-
ities, such as the invention of mathematics; the ability
to engage in engineering design; and the capacity to
write, comprehend, and enjoy novels and dramas. It
also follows that meta-representations (the represen-
tation of mental representations) is a key requirement
for the new mind for the following reason. You only
need mental representation to believe something, but
you need meta-representation to suppose something.
And such suppositions must be represented explicitly,
so that they are distinguished from actual beliefs.

In application to reasoning, decision making, and
social judgment I call the old mind “intuitive” and
the new mind “reﬂective” (Evans, 2010). Autonomous
Type 1 systems operating through the old mind have the
potential to control behavior directly and frequently do
so, as when I make an habitual drive to work with my
reﬂective mind freed up to think about the day’s work
ahead. However, the new mind always has the potential
to intervene, as happens when a hazardous trafﬁc situ-
ation is suddenly encountered. In the case of judgment
and decision making, we call a behavioral propensity
emanating from the old mind an intuitive judgement.
If we rely on our intuition, “we” (our brains) do what
feels right without reﬂecting on it or allocating any
precious working memory resources. However, these
intuitions do post conscious feelings, which enable the
new mind to intervene on default intuitions just as it
may on default driving behavior. Whether or not we
actually engage reﬂective reasoning depends upon a
whole host of factors. These are best illustrated by ex-
amining some of the literature on dual processing.
Intuition in Reasoning Tasks
Some psychologists working in the ﬁeld of deduc-
tive reasoning appear to assume that their enterprise is
one of studying the process of explicit reasoning: the
manipulation of rules and facts to derive conclusions.
I am not and have never been one of them. I started my
ﬁrst text book on the ﬁeld with these words:
In one sense this book is only about deductive rea-
soning. In another sense it is about language compre-
hension, mental imagery, learning processes, memory
organization and the nature of human thought. The
ﬁrst sense is deﬁned by the paradigms employed; the
second by the natures of the psychological processes
which the paradigms evoke. (Evans, 1982, p. 1)
Although it is true that those engaged in studying
the psychology of reasoning are generally interested
in explicit reasoning processes, it is also evident, as
I demonstrated in this early review, that that many
different kinds of cognitive processes are brought to
bear on performance of the tasks that are used in an
attempt to study reasoning. And it turns out that many
of these are implicit or intuitive in nature. So the title
of this section is not as paradoxical as it may seem:
The attempt to study reasoning has actually led us to
learn a lot about intuition.
The deduction paradigm proper involves present-
ing people with logical arguments comprised of one
or more premises and a conclusion that may or may
not follow (a variant involves asking participants to
generate their own conclusions). It is conventional to
use participants who have had no formal training in
logic and to instruct them (a) to assume the truth of the
premises and (b) to endorse (or generate) only conclu-
sions which necessarily follow from the premises. The
historical origin of this rather strange practice is clear:
a strong philosophical and psychological tradition that
assumes that people must have some in-built capac-
ity for logical reasoning to be rational thinkers (see
Evans, 2002). Thus the paradigm tests people’s ability
to reasoning logically without having been trained to
do so. An intensive use of the paradigm between around
1960 and 2000 led to the following ﬁndings: (a) Peo-
ple are very poor at solving such reasoning tasks, (b)
they exhibit many cognitive biases, and (c) reasoning is
highly content dependent. Although early researchers,
like Peter Wason, inferred that people were irrational,
the longer term view developed that logicism itself was
mistaken: Logic cannot be the basis for rationality in
an uncertain world (Evans, 2002; Evans & Over, 1996;
Oaksford & Chater, 1998; Oaksford & Chater, 2007).
Working in this ﬁeld myself since the 1970s, I was
less interested in arguments about how deduction might
be achieved (e.g., rules vs. mental models) and more in-
terested in understanding exactly what people were do-
ing on these tasks. A key publication was that of Evans,
Barston, and Pollard (1983), often cited as the founda-
tion for the modern study of belief bias in reasoning.
In fact, belief bias was ﬁrst demonstrated by Wilkins
(1928), but our article established the modern paradigm
and the main ﬁndings. Participants were given classical
syllogisms comprising two premises and a conclusion,
relating three terms. They were given full deductive
reasoning instructions (assume the premises, draw nec-
essary conclusions), and the syllogisms used fell into
four categories depending upon whether the conclusion
followed necessarily from the premises (valid/invalid)
and whether the conclusion presented was believable or
unbelievable. Examples are shown in Table 1, together,
with the observed rates at which people endorsed their
conclusions.
Three main ﬁndings observed by Evans et al. (1983)
have been replicated in many later studies (e.g., Klauer,
Musch, & Naumer, 2000) and can be seen in Table 1.
First, people endorse more valid than invalid conclu-
sions, as you would expect if they were reasoning log-
ically. Second, and contrary to the instructions given,
they endorse more believable than unbelievable con-
clusions: the belief bias effect. Third, the belief bias
is stronger on invalid syllogisms, leading to a signif-
icant belief by logic interaction. Evans et al. showed
that the logic-belief conﬂict was within participants
and the same person might resolve it different ways
on different trials. This was one of the key ﬁndings
in the development of dual-process accounts of rea-
soning in which the validity effect was attributed to
Type 2 reasoning and the belief bias effect to a Type 1
intuition. However, more recent analyses (Evans, Han-

Is there direct evidence that the Type 1 belief bias is
intuitive? There are several ﬁndings that suggest that
it is. Recall that one deﬁnition of intuition is that it
is immediate. Evans and Curtis-Holmes (2005) used
a speeded task method in which participants were re-
quired to respond in less than half the time they would
normally take. This altered the usual pattern of results
in three ways: (a) The validity effect was greatly re-
duced, (b) the belief bias effect was increased, and
(c) the Belief × Logic interaction disappeared. All
three ﬁndings are consistent with the hypothesis that
the speeded task enforces reliance on intuition, thus
enhancing the Type 1 belief bias and inhibiting both
logical and motivated (Type 2) reasoning. Similar ﬁnd-
ings have been reported by De Neys (2006) when par-
ticipants were asked to reason with a concurrent work-
ing memory load. He also found better performance
on belief–logic conﬂict problems for participants of
higher working memory capacity.
In a recent study, Shynkarkuk and Thompson (2006)
directly investigated the role of intuition in syllogis-
tic reasoning. Participants were instructed to give two
answers: the ﬁrst that came to mind within a short
time limit and a second given after a free period of
reﬂection. Participants also rated their subjective feel-
ings of conﬁdence in their answers. Participants were
more conﬁdent in their second answers, after a pe-
riod of reﬂection, but not more accurate. In fact, there
was no relation at all between conﬁdence and logical
accuracy in this study. This contrasts with studies of
meta-cognition and memory, in which conﬁdence rat-
ings normally have a positive correlation with accuracy
(Koriat, 1993). Moreover, people were more conﬁdent
in their conclusions about belief-laden materials, which
we know to causes belief biases, than in syllogisms
with abstract content. These ﬁndings suggest that intu-
ition plays a strong role in reasoning experiments, but
an unhelpful one.
In a recent study, Morsanyi and Handley (2010)
presented syllogistic problems and simply asked par-
ticipants to rate how much they liked their conclusions.
The results were remarkably similar to those observed
on the standard task with the usual main effects and
interaction. This applied whether or not participants
were instructed respond intuitively on the basis of their
feelings, or on the basis of how a person reasoning log-
ically would respond. What is striking here is that peo-
ple like valid conclusions as well as believable ones.
Is validity in some sense intuitive also? That it may
be is suggested by recent ﬁndings that physiological
or neurological indicators may signal conﬂict detec-
tion even when the belief bias dominates (De Neys,
Moyens, & Vansteenwegen, 2010; see also De Neys
et al., 2008, for a related ﬁnding on a different task).
This is a theoretical puzzle, because you cannot know
there is a conﬂict without knowing whether the argu-
ment is valid, something generally assumed to require
Type 2 reasoning. However, it has also been suggested
that syllogisms provide heuristic cues that are highly
correlated with validity (Chater & Oaksford, 2004).
Peter Wason, the founder of the modern psychol-
ogy of reasoning, demonstrated how cognitive biases
in reasoning could be intuitively compelling. In the “2
4 6” task (Wason, 1960), participants are asked to work
out a rule that the experimenter has in mind which clas-
siﬁes “triples” of three whole numbers. They are given
an example that conforms to the rule—2 4 6—and
asked to generate triples of their own: In each case the
experimenter tells them whether the generated triples
conform or not. Typically, people test hypotheses like
“ascending with equal intervals” and generate positive
examples: 10 20 30; 1 2 3; 34 36 38; and so on, in
each case receiving positive feedback. But when they
announce the rule they are told they are wrong and
have to carry on. The actual rule is any ascending se-
quence. Participants get very frustrated and often go

Wason (1966) also devised a reasoning problem,
known as the four card selection task, which was in-
tensively studied for the next 30 years or so. In this case
participants are given a conditional statement such as,
“If there is an A on one side of the card then there is a
3 on the other side of the card,” and asked to try to dis-
cover whether it is true or false. They are told it applies
to cards that have a letter on one side and a number on
the other and are shown four such cards, with exposed
faces that show A, D, 3, 7. They are instructed to turn
over those cards and only those cards that could prove
whether the rule is true or false. Most people choose A
and 3 (sometimes just A), although the correct answer
according to standard logic is A and 7. They must try to
ﬁnd a card that has an A on one side and does not have
a 3 on the other, which would falsify the statement.
Only the A and 7 (not a 3) could do this.
Wason’s original explanation was a veriﬁcation
bias, which implies faulty Type 2 reasoning. However,
he retracted this when I demonstrated a matching bias
on the task. If the rule is changed to “If there is an A on
one side of the card then there is NOT a 3 on the other
side of the card,” participants still choose A and 3, the
cards matching the values in the statement, and they
are now logically correct (Evans & Lynch, 1973; for a
review of much subsequent research, see Evans, 1998).
For whatever reason, the matching cards are intuitively
compelling, suggesting that card choices are under con-
trol of Type 1 processes. Participants will come up with
rationalizations of these choices in the context of the
instructions whether or not the negation is present (Lu-
cas & Ball, 2005; Wason & Evans, 1975), arguing that
they are verifying or falsifying the statement as applies
in either case. Later research showed that people focus
their attention selectively on matching cards (Ball, Lu-
cas, Miles, & Gale, 2003; Evans, 1996). Only a small
minority of people with very high general intelligence
appear to be able to overcome matching bias and at-
tend to the 7 card on the standard problem (Stanovich
& West, 1998). However, recent analyses suggest that
participants do reason about the cards to which they
attend, often leading them to withhold choice of an
irrelevant card, even when it matches (Evans & Ball,
2010).
When the selection task uses realistic materials and
is placed in a familiar context, it is often much easier to
solve. For example, if you are told to play the role of a
police ofﬁcer checking that the following rule is being
obeyed, “If people are drinking alcohol then they must
be over 18 years of age,” the four cards can be used to
represent four drinkers. One side shows the beverage
and the other side the age of the drinker. Given the
cards “beer,” “Coke,” “21 years of age,” and “16 years
of age,” most people will choose to investigate the beer
drinker and the underage drinker, which is logically
correct and not subject to matching bias (Griggs &
Cox, 1982). Does this mean that people have switched
from faulty intuition to good logical reasoning? Not so.
The evidence suggests that they have switched from
faulty intuition to good intuition, as their experience
of permission and obligation rules provides helpful
cues. On such rules, attention is directed from the start
toward the correct cards (Evans, 1996) and there is
little relation between these choices and measures of
cognitive ability (Stanovich & West, 1998). It has even
been argued that solving such problems requires no
reasoning at all (Sperber & Girotto, 2002).
This brief survey of the psychology of reasoning
allows us to draw a most interesting conclusion. The
performance of tasks speciﬁcally designed to look for
explicit Type 2 reasoning are often strongly inﬂuenced,
or even dominated by, Type 1 intuitive processes. This
ﬁnding is quite remarkable when you consider that
(a) these tasks routinely include instructions designed
to engage effortful and systematic reasoning and (b)
most employ university students of above population
average intelligence as their participants. It compels us
to the conclusion that intuition rather than reasoning is
the dominant form of human thinking. Although such
intuitions can lead to correct answers, they more often
generate cognitive biases as well as inspiring feelings
of conﬁdence in the wrong answers.
Why does reasoning apparently play so little part
in reasoning experiments? One view is that reasoning
evolved for the purposes of argumentation (Mercier
& Sperber, in press) and hence is more often used
to construct justiﬁcations for intuitive responses than
to generate the answers themselves. However, even if
reasoning did evolve in that way it may have been
“exapted” for another purpose. It seems impossible
to explain the unique intellectual, technological, and
cultural achievements of the human species without
assuming that genuine Type 2 thinking can underlie
our problem solving and decision making. Stanovich
(2009c) certainly believes so but has suggested that
people are by nature “cognitive misers” who minimize
the deployment of effortful reasoning, hence often rely-
ing on intuition. Although this may often be successful
where we have relevant experiential learning, the rea-
soning literature shows that it can also have potentially
disastrous consequences for people required to reason
in a modern technological world. Stanovich stressed
that the inclination to reason is related to rational think-
ing disposition rather than general intelligence, which
instead predicts the accuracy of any reasoning that gets
applied. This suggests the importance of education and
training in persuading people, where appropriate, to
reason explicitly rather than to rely on intuition.

Intuition in Judgment and Decision Making
Everyday experience suggests that decisions can be
made both intuitively and reﬂectively. For example, if
a friend asks you what you would like to drink, the
answer will often just appear instantly as what you feel
like on that occasion. Of course, you may also engage
in reﬂection: You may feel like a gin and tonic but set-
tle for an orange juice as you will be driving your car
shortly. This example seems similar to the notion of
Type 2 reasoning overriding a default Type 1 intuition
in the reasoning research previously discussed. It also
illustrates what I termed new mind rationality earlier
in this article: Long-term goals and anticipated con-
sequences may take over from immediate satisfaction
of desires. Such overrides in everyday decision mak-
ing occur frequently: the student with a hangover who
wants to stay in bed but gets up to attend a lecture,
the married person who resists a tempting sexual op-
portunity, the dieter who refuses a favorite food, and
so on. Intuitive decision making need not arise from
immediate desires, however, and may instead reﬂect
lazy thinking or cognitive biases as in many of the
examples already discussed in the reasoning ﬁeld. As
the earlier cited work of Frederick (2005) illustrates,
people well capable of applying some basic reasoning
to a problem may nevertheless rely on immediate and
faulty intuitions.
The theory of rational decision making, which was
imported into psychology from the discipline of eco-
nomics, requires that a rational person should antic-
ipate the consequences of their decisions, estimating
the probability and utility of various outcomes, com-
bining the two to calculate the expected utility of each
action, and then choosing the action that maximizes
this quantity (von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944; in-
troduced to psychology by Edwards, 1954, 1961). In
psychological terms, this requires sophisticated hypo-
thetical thinking: The individual must carry out mental
simulations of the future consequences of all possi-
ble actions. For many years, psychological research on
decision making was focused on demonstrating vio-
lations of decision theory rather than elucidating the
underlying cognitive processes (for reviews of rele-
vant literature, see D. J. Koehler & Harvey, 2004). The
famous prospect theory article by Kahneman and Tver-
sky (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; see also Tversky &
Kahneman, 1992) demonstrated numerous violations
of expected utility as well as proposing an alternative
descriptive account of risky choice.
One reason that people may fail to maximize ex-
pected utility is that they are not actually think-
ing consequentially, that is, people do not engage in
sufﬁcient reasoning about the consequences of their
choices. More cognitively oriented laboratory research
has demonstrated this directly (Baron, 1994), whereas
studies of expert decision making in the ﬁeld also found
little evidence of classically rational decision making
(Klein, 1999). However, in the latter case it is often ob-
served that the correct course of action comes to mind
immediately for an expert decision maker. Where rel-
evant experiential learning has taken place, intuition
may be a reliable basis for decision making in many
cases. Klein (1999) also observed, however, that when
problems are novel or unusual, explicit mental simu-
lations may be carried out. I have argued with many
examples (Evans, 2007) that when people do conduct
such mental simulations they tend to focus on a single
hypothesis, unless there is good reason to give it up.
This explains many phenomena, including the perse-
verance with a faulty hypothesis on the Wason 2 4 6
problem, discussed earlier. I call this the “singularity
principle” of hypothetical thinking.
In the reasoning research discussed earlier, I showed
that even when people are reasoning, their focus may
be restricted by preconscious intuitive processes. For
example, people reason about the cards to which they
attend on the selection task, but attention is cued by
intuitive processes like matching bias. They may apply
reasoning to try to prove a believable conclusion or to
disprove an unbelievable one, but not vice versa. In
the same way, there are many examples of restricted
attention in decision making tasks. Legrenzi, Girotto
and Johnson-Laird (1993) noted that when asked to
decide whether they would spend an evening in a par-
ticular way, people do not request to know what alter-
natives are available. Instead, they focus on the case
given, for example, whether to attend an expensive but
high-quality opera, an apparent case of the singularity
principle. Many studies have also shown that the way
in which the experimenter frames a decision problem
may have dramatic impact on the choices taken (see
Keren, 2010). For example, asking people to choose
to accept, or instead reject, one of two candidates will
focus them on either positive or negative reasons for
their choice, leading to inconsistent decisions (Shaﬁr,
Simenson, & Tversky, 1993). So, again, intuitive and
pragmatic processes rapidly provide the focus for rea-
soning, affecting its outcome.
Psychologists have been particular interested in de-
cisions made under risk, which require estimation of
probabilities and utilities associated with particular
outcomes. Because these typically deviate from ex-
pected utility, much work has focused on judgments
of subjective probabilities and how they are formed.
In a famous series of papers published from the early
1970s onwards, Amos Tversky and Danny Kahneman
proposed that such judgments are by reliance on heuris-
tics, or shortcut rules of thumb. Because such heuris-
tics were typically blamed for a number of observed
cognitive biases, this became known as the “heuris-
tics and biases” research program (Gilovich, Grifﬁn,
& Kahneman, 2002; Kahneman, Slovic, & Tversky,
1982). For example, reliance on a representativeness

heuristic was blamed for neglect of sample size (Kah-
neman & Tversky, 1972), neglect of base rates (Kah-
neman & Tversky, 1973) and the conjunction fallacy
(Tversky & Kahneman, 1983). A judgment by repre-
sentativeness is essentially one based on similarity of
salient features. For example, when asked to judge the
likelihood that a sample is drawn from a particular
population, people tend to compare their mean scores,
neglecting other statistically relevant features, such as
sample size.
This research program is controversial and for our
purposes also has an important ambiguity. The two
issues are (a) are heuristics good or bad, and (b) are
they based on explicit (Type 2) or implicit (Type 1)
processes? Let me consider these separately. Kahne-
man and Tversky’s work was widely cited as show-
ing evidence of human irrationality, leading to a joint
attack on their work and the early studies of Peter
Wason (Cohen, 1981) from a rationalist perspective.
Their work was also criticized by Gerd Gigerzner (e.g.,
Gigerenzer, 1991), who subsequently developed a re-
search program of his own in which the adaptive value
of heuristics was stressed (Gigerenzer, Todd, & the
ABC Research Group, 1999) known as the “fast and
frugal heuristics” program. A neutral view might be
that heuristics are neither good nor bad. As originally
pointed by Newell and Simon (1972), they are shortcut
solutions to complex problems that may save enormous
amounts of processing effort but that are not guaran-
teed to succeed. Thus we might see these two programs
as focusing on cases where heuristics are useful, on one
hand, and lead to biases, on the other.
The more pertinent question for our purposes is
(b). Are heuristics Type 1 or Type 2? In the reason-
ing literature, the term “heuristic processes” is often
used as a synonym for Type 1 or intuitive process-
ing. On the other hand, an heuristic can be thought
of as a simple but explicit rule that someone can fol-
low deliberately. The early publications on heuristics
were quite ambiguous in this respect. For example,
the availability heuristic (Schwarz & Vaughn, 2002;
Tversky & Kahneman, 1973) is deﬁned as judging the
probability of an event by the ease with which items
come to mind. This could be intuitive: We might feel
that events are probable because they easily activate
our memory system. It could also be reﬂective: We
might reason that events easily recalled must have oc-
curred more frequently. Both Kahneman and Gigeren-
zer seem to agree that heuristics are Type 1. Kahneman
and Frederick (2002) retrospectively adopted dual pro-
cess theory (but see Kahneman & Tversky, 1982, for
a forerunner), attributing biases to System 1 that may
or may not be overridden by explicit System 2 reason-
ing. By the latter device, they explain in common with
Stanovich (1999, 2010) why participants may some-
times avoid the dominant bias and ﬁnd a normative
solution. Gigerenzer is a well-known critic of dual-
process theory (e.g., Gigerenzer & Regier, 1996), but
his advocacy of reliance on “gut feelings” in judgment
(Gigerenzer, 2007) nevertheless suggests that he sees
heuristics as intuitive.
I am inclined to agree with Betsch (2008) that these
literatures confuse two different kinds of judgment,
which he calls intuitive and heuristic. Intuitive judge-
ment is based on feelings of rightness or conﬁdence and
is therefore Type 1. Heuristic judgement, on the other
hand, is based on simple but explicit rules and is there-
fore Type 2. Although in general Type 1 processing is
quicker than Type 2, it is nonetheless a fallacy to be-
lieve that a process is necessarily Type 1 just because it
is quick (Evans, in press). In common with Gigernzer,
Gladwell (2005) adopted the jingle “less is more” to ad-
vocate the merits of intuitive judgment. But this phrase
is ambiguous: What exactly is less? The time taken?
The amount of information processed? The effort re-
quired? The ﬁrst case that Gladwell discussed in his
book is of an art expert who immediately “knows” that
a statute is a fake but is unable to prove it for some
time after. This is clearly a case of intuitive judgment.
However, a later example of “less is more” is that of
a hospital emergency room examining patients with
chest pains. He shows how a hospital that replaced a
complex series of diagnostic tests with just three simple
questions achieved better results in trying to determine
whether the patient should be treated as having a sus-
pected heart attack. But this is clearly heuristic and not
intuitive judgement. Explicit rule following is a Type
2 process however short and simple the rules may be.
One of the most widely investigated phenomena in-
spired by the heuristics and biases programme is often
known as the “base rate fallacy.” According to Bayes’
theorem, which is a direct consequence of the probabil-
ity calculus, posterior judgements about a hypothesis
given some evidence should depend on two multiplica-
tive factors: (a) the prior probability of the hypothesis
and (b) the diagnosticity of the evidence, that is the
extent to which it favors the hypothesis under con-
sideration relative to rival hypotheses. For example, a
positive medical test for a disease might be a hundred
times more likely to occur in someone with the disease
than without it. But if the base rate probability of hav-
ing the disease in the population is 1 in 1,000, then it
is still very likely to be false positive result. Early re-
search by Kahneman and Tversky (1973) showed that
people will focus strongly on the diagnostic informa-
tion to the neglect of the base rate or prior probability,
a ﬁnding that they attributed to the representativeness
heuristic. Due to simpliﬁed and inaccurate citation,
this led to myth that people ignore base rates, whereas
the evidence shows that they underweight them (J. J.
Koehler, 1996). However, it is generally agreed that
even the relative neglect of base rates is an important
cognitive bias affecting decision making in medicine
and other real-world applications.

Correct Bayesian reasoning is really difﬁcult to
achieve for someone not trained in statistics. Even
participants of higher cognitive ability neglect base
rates unless pragmatic cues to their relevance are pro-
vided (Stanovich, 1999). You need both to somehow
derive Bayes’ theorem, which is not given by the exper-
imenter, and to do the arithmetic necessary to combine
base rates and diagnostic data. People are generally
good at it only when probabilities are both presented
as frequencies and arranged in “nested sets” (Barbey
& Sloman, 2007; Cosmides & Tooby, 1996; Gigeren-
zer & Hoffrage, 1995; Hoffrage, Gigerenzer, Krauss,
& Martigon, 2002). For example, if you are told that
out of 1,000 people, 1 will have the disease and test
positive but 50 healthy people will also test positive,
then people have little difﬁculty in saying (correctly)
that a person with a positive result is only about 2%
likely to have the disease. If instead you are simply told
that the false positive rate for a medical test is 1 in 20
and the base rate for the condition is 1 in 1,000, peo-
ple most commonly say that a person with a positive
test results is 95% likely to have the disease! (Cos-
mides & Tooby, 1996; Evans, Handley, Perham, Over,
& Thompson, 2000). The nested sets presentation ap-
pears to cue a mental model that makes the logical
relationships transparent and thus facilitates Type 2
reasoning.
Although the intuitive response to the complex ver-
sion of this problem is typically based on the diagnostic
information, there is still some (fallacious) reasoning
involved. People must argue something like, “The test
has a false positive rate of 1 in 20, which means it
is right 95% of the time.” Moreover, as has not often
been noted in this literature, these studies also showed
that a minority of participants give the answer 1 in
1,000, thus matching the base rate. What they are ap-
parently unable to do is to integrate the two pieces of
information. Clearly, the standard probability presen-
tation of the Bayesian reasoning problem fails to meet
a key criterion for the intervention of good reasoning:
Participants must have the “mindware” for the task
(Stanovich, 2010). Without the relevant education, no
one carries Bayes’ theorem around in their heads. But
why do most people focus on the diagnostic rather than
base rate information? My colleagues and I hypothe-
sised that this was due to a bias in the normal format of
the task. In many studies the diagnostic data are more
intuitive because they are based on stereotypes and ex-
isting beliefs, whereas prior probabilities are conveyed
only by explicit base rate information. We managed to
devise a task in which this was switched around: Base
rates were conveyed by genuine prior beliefs and diag-
nostic information by statistics (Evans, Handley, Over,
& Perham, 2002). This shifted the balance of power
and in one experiment led to base rates dominating the
judgments. So we can see that a form of belief bias
operates in this paradigm.
The correct conclusion to draw from research on
Bayesian reasoning and base rate neglect is that this is
not a problem that can be reliably solved by intuition,
not because people are stupid or irrational but simply
because they do not have the relevant rules available
without training. Of course, it then follows that where
good Bayesian reasoning is required for professional
expertise, such training should be provided. This in-
cludes not only doctors but those in other professions
who sometimes need statistical reasoning, for exam-
ple, lawyers and judges who are required to interpret
DNA evidence, which can easily result in fallacious
reasoning in the untrained (Gigerenzer, 2002).
The judgment and decision literature has lagged be-
hind the psychology of reasoning in the adoption of
process-tracing measures such as response latencies,
verbal protocols, and neural-imaging as well as the use
of other dual-process manipulations such as working
memory load, speeded tasks, and correlate with cog-
nitive ability measures. For this reason, it is not easy
to sort out what in these literatures is genuinely intu-
itive in the sense of being based on Type 1 processing.
However, there is recent evidence that supports Kah-
neman and Frederick’s (2002) proposal that heuristics
and biases should be interpreted within a dual-process
framework. Take the case of the conjunction fallacy,
famously illustrated by the Linda problem (Tversky &
Kahneman, 1983). Participants are given a brief de-
scription of Linda as follows:
Linda is 31 years old, single, outspoken, and very
bright. She majored in philosophy. As a student, she
was deeply concerned with issues of discrimination
and social justice, and also participated in antinuclear
demonstrations.
When asked to rank a series of statements about
Linda, the original study showed that, on average, par-
ticipants rated “Linda is a bank teller and is active in
the feminist movement” as signiﬁcantly more likely
than “Linda is a bank teller.” However, this amounts
to an assertion that P(A&B) > P(A), which cannot be
true for any A and B by the laws of probability (and
those of simple logic). On the face of it this seems
a clear bias of intuitive judgment. On the description
given you would not expect Linda to be a bank teller
but you would expect her to be a feminist. When the
feminist description is added, the statement feels more
probable.
What has emerged in recent research is that perfor-
mance on the Linda problem and a number of other
decision problems is better among those of higher cog-
nitive ability, but only if a within participant design
is used (Stanovich & West, 2008). In the between-
participant design, individuals simply rate the proba-
bility of one or the other statement, and the conjunction
will be rated higher, regardless of cognitive ability.

Why should this be? Stanovich (2010) agreed with
Kahneman and Frederick (2002) that ﬁnding the cor-
rect solution on problems like this requires “System
2” intervention. However, he has reﬁned his earlier
claims that such interventions are more likely to oc-
cur in those of higher ability (Stanovich, 1999). This
is now viewed as only one of several requirements for
default intuitions to be overridden by reasoning. One,
as previously mentioned, is possession of the relevant
“mindware.” Another is the perceived need for reason-
ing, which may be inﬂuenced by a number of factors
such as instructions given, feelings of conﬁdence in
the intuitive answer, and the rational thinking dispo-
sition of the individual. The point about the within-
participant design is that individuals can see that they
are wanting to assign a higher value to P(A&B) than
to P(A), which may cue the need for reasoning and
the detection of the inconsistency. This now makes
it comparable to a belief bias experiment in which
high-ability participants will inhibit beliefs but only
if given strict deductive reasoning instructions of the
kind described earlier (Evans, Handley, Neilens, Ba-
con, & Over, 2010). Hence, intervention on intuitions
by reasoning requires both the cognitive capacity for
the relevant reasoning and the awareness of the need for
doing so.
If one assumes the perspective of the normative the-
ory that is built into our research paradigms, then the
psychology of reasoning appears to be unconnected
with the study of judgment and decision making. The
former assess people’s ability to make certain deduc-
tions from assumed premises; the latter ﬁeld investi-
gates people’s ability to choose actions by calculating
and comparing their consequences. The psychological
reality, however, is quite different. The two research
ﬁelds have similar methods and similar ﬁndings. Re-
searchers in both ﬁelds give participants problems to
solve in the laboratory for which they lack any requi-
site training. Most people (consciously or not) rely on
intuitive feelings about the answers and frequently get
them wrong. A small number of exceptionally intel-
ligent participants manage to reason out the answers
from ﬁrst principles, but even then they usually need
help in the form of instructions or structural clues in
the problem to get them on the right track. Perhaps the
approach is fundamentally misconceived. One could
argue that we will learn more about rationality by
studying people’s ability to apply rule-based reason-
ing that they have been taught, rather than by con-
stantly studying the performance of na¨ıve participants
on novel problems.
Conclusions
The case for distinctively human powers of reason-
ing and reﬂective thinking that underlie the unique
achievements of our species seems beyond doubt.
However, in reviewing the case for the two minds hy-
pothesis (Evans, 2010), I was struck over and over by
the dominance of intuition in control of our behavior.
The belief that we have in ourselves as conscious con-
trolling agents does seem to be largely illusory. When
we have a severe phobia, insight into the condition may
not help us to escape it; if we have a gambling habit,
our powers of reasoning merely serve to rationalize our
behavior and confabulate erroneous theories of chance
events. As I have shown in this article, when we study
na¨ıve reasoning and decision making in the laboratory,
immediate, intuitive, and effort-free answers strongly
compete with those based on reﬂective reasoning, often
resulting in cognitive biases.
Although historically, philosophers and other schol-
ars have stressed the importance of (logical) reasoning
to achieve rationality, there has been a recent fashion
for emphasising instead the value of reliance on in-
tuition. From a dual-process perspective, neither view
is correct. The evidence suggests that intuition is the
dominant basis for real world decision making and is
often effective; however, it also shows that reliance on
intuition can be dangerous and that intervention with
high-effort and explicit reasoning is often required,
especially when problems have novel features. The
problem with doing what feels right is that one has
no reasoned basis for one’s actions. This problem is
greatly exacerbated by our tendency to reason after the
event, confabulating justiﬁcations for our intuitions and
constructing an illusion of conscious control.
Although the value of focusing on na¨ıve participants
and novel problems in the study of reasoning and de-
cision making is debatable, this practice has taught us
some important lessons. The ﬁrst and most obvious is
that people are not good at ﬁnding normative solutions
to problems in logic, probability, and decision theory
without special training. The massive evidence of cog-
nitive biases on these tasks needs to be explained, for
these are systematic and not random errors. Although
some of these can be attributed to limits in working
memory capacity and in reasoning strategy, many turn
out to reﬂect reliance on intuition. Moreover, these
intuitions are convincing to the individual—they feel
right, they have conﬁdence in them, and they frequently
deter them from more effortful reasoning. Worse still,
we can always ﬁnd a rational sounding explanation for
our actions.
Why is it that intuition so often dominates reason-
ing? I think there are two answers to this. The ﬁrst is
that intuitive feelings largely reﬂect experiential learn-
ing. In the real world, dealing with familiar environ-
ments, intuition will often serve us well. The second
reason is to do with fundamental cognitive architec-
ture of the human mind. Although intuitive processes
operate rapidly, in parallel and with no effort, reﬂec-
tive reasoning is quite the opposite. Reﬂection requires

use of central working memory: There is only one
such system—it has low capacity, requires high effort,
and can be applied only to one task at a time. We are
miserly with this cognitive resource because we have
to be. The task of psychologists is to identity when
intuition can safely be relied upon and when it cannot.
From an educational viewpoint, we need not just to
ﬁnd ways of training people to reason well. We also
need to ﬁnd ways of making people aware of the sit-
